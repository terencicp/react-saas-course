A B2B customer has a daily report wired to fire at "9 AM Eastern." It works all spring and summer. Then, on the morning of November 1, 2026, the report lands at 8 AM their time, and they file a ticket. You read the code and nothing is wrong: the cron expression still says nine, the timezone still says Eastern, and the job ran when it always ran. What shifted was the *wall clock* underneath it. On that date, Eastern time fell back an hour, and a schedule that ignored the shift drifted by sixty minutes.

Earlier lessons stored time that had already happened: an invoice's `createdAt`, a `dueDate`, the timezone on a user's profile. Scheduling is about time that *hasn't happened yet*, and that future is where <Term definition="Daylight Saving Time — the twice-yearly shift of the wall clock, observed in some regions and not others. Because it isn't universal, a fixed UTC offset like -05:00 can't model it.">DST</Term> bites. You already hold both halves of the fix: the `users.timeZone` column from the profile lesson and Trigger.dev's `schedules` from the background-work chapter. The rule that connects them is this: a job whose fire time means something to a human in a place names a timezone, and a job that just needs a cadence runs in UTC.

## What DST does to a wall clock

A DST-observing zone has two transitions a year, and they do opposite things.

**Spring forward** happens in the US on March 8, 2026: at 2 AM the clock jumps straight to 3 AM, so the hour from 2:00 to 2:59 *does not exist* that day. Ask for 2:30 AM and there is no real instant to point at. **Fall back** happens on November 1, 2026: at 2 AM the clock drops back to 1 AM, so the hour from 1:00 to 1:59 *happens twice*, making 1:30 AM two different instants an hour apart. This is why DST is a scheduling problem, not just a formatting curiosity: a job set for 2:30 AM in the spring gap has nowhere to fire, and a job set for 1:30 AM in the fall repeat has two places to fire and no rule for choosing.

The rule follows directly: **never schedule a recurring job in the 1 AM to 3 AM window of a DST-observing zone.** Nine in the morning, noon, and five in the afternoon are never inside a gap or a repeat, so they can't drift and can't double-fire, which sidesteps most DST pain. The rest of this lesson is for the times you can't avoid that window, or when you construct a wall-clock time yourself instead of letting the scheduler do it.

The principle underneath: **DST is a property of the calendar, not the timestamp.** A `Temporal.Instant` is just a count of nanoseconds from a fixed epoch, so it carries no wall clock and is never ambiguous. Ambiguity appears only when you attach a wall-clock time *and* a zone together, which is exactly what a `Temporal.ZonedDateTime` is. So all of DST's hard math lives in one type, and the scheduler owns most of it for you.

:::note
"Eastern" isn't a real timezone you can hand a computer. It's a colloquial name for two offsets: EST in winter and EDT in summer. The thing you actually name is an <Term definition="A named zone like America/New_York from the IANA Time Zone Database. Unlike a bare offset such as -05:00, it carries the zone's full set of past and future DST rules — so it knows when, and by how much, the wall clock shifts.">IANA timezone</Term> like `America/New_York`, which carries the full rulebook of when that region springs forward and falls back. That rulebook is what lets the scheduler turn "9 AM Eastern" into the correct real instant on any date.
:::

## Name a zone, or run in UTC

A recurring job can be expressed three ways, and two of them are really the same answer.

**Option A: schedule in UTC and never touch it.** You pick a fixed UTC time, and `0 13 * * *` fires at 13:00 UTC every day. But 13:00 UTC is 9 AM in New York in *winter* and 8 AM in *summer*, because the UTC-to-Eastern offset shifts when DST flips. The customer's wall-clock time moves by an hour twice a year. For a report read with morning coffee, that's the bug from the introduction; for a job no human watches, it's fine.

**Option B: schedule a wall-clock expression plus a named IANA zone.** You tell the scheduler "9 AM in `America/New_York`," and it recomputes the next real instant from that zone's DST rules every time. "9 AM Eastern" stays 9 AM Eastern all year: the scheduler fires at 13:00 UTC in winter and 14:00 UTC in summer, so the wall clock the customer sees never moves. This is the default for anything a human reads at a local time.

**Option C: trigger on a UTC cron and recompute the local time yourself.** It works, but it's strictly more code for Option B's result, so reach for it never; Option B already does this for you.

Collapse the three into one rule:

> **User-facing and wall-clock-meaningful → name the user's or org's zone. Internal cadence → UTC.**

Route any job with a single gut-check, the same kind the calendar-days lesson used to tell a `date` from a `timestamptz`: *"Would a human be upset if this ran an hour off?"* If yes, as with a 9 AM report, a billing-cutoff reminder, or a "your trial ends today" email, it's wall-clock-meaningful, so name a zone. If no, as with a retention sweep, a metric rollup, or a cache warm, only its <Term definition="How often a job runs, independent of what wall-clock time it happens to land on. When only the frequency matters, UTC is correct — there's no human reading the clock.">cadence</Term> matters, and UTC is correct.

A third answer belongs to neither column and ships silently: letting the server's `TZ` decide. Write a wall-clock schedule without a zone and the runtime fills in its own default, which differs by machine. Your laptop runs your local zone; Vercel runs `TZ=UTC`. So an un-zoned "9 AM" means "9 AM Eastern in dev, 9 AM UTC in prod": it passes every local test, then misfires on deploy. The cure is one habit: **always pass the timezone explicitly, even when it's UTC.**

## Wiring a static schedule with `schedules.task`

The simplest shape is the *static* schedule from the background-work chapter: one global schedule, declared in the task file, that the platform runs for you. Here `cron` is an **object**, `{ pattern, timezone }`, and naming the zone is what keeps "9 AM Eastern" at 9 AM Eastern across both DST transitions.

The new piece is the *seam*. The `run` payload hands you a `Date`, the type you've spent this chapter keeping out of your domain, so you convert it the moment it arrives and the `Date` travels no further. Same discipline as database rows and Stripe payloads: `Date` at the seam, `Temporal` in the domain.

The example is a weekday 9 AM Eastern summary.

<AnnotatedCode lang="ts" maxLines={14} code={`
import { schedules } from '@trigger.dev/sdk';
import { instantFromDate } from '@/lib/temporal';
import { buildSummaryFor } from '@/lib/reports';

export const weeklySummary = schedules.task({
  id: 'weekly-summary',
  cron: {
    pattern: '0 9 * * 1-5',
    timezone: 'America/New_York',
  },
  run: async (payload) => {
    const fireInstant = instantFromDate(payload.timestamp);
    await buildSummaryFor({ instant: fireInstant });
  },
});
`}>
	<AnnotatedStep meta="{7-10}" color="green">
		The object form of `cron`: a `pattern` plus an explicit `timezone`. That one field makes the schedule DST-safe. You name the zone and let the scheduler do the spring-forward and fall-back math, rather than computing UTC times by hand. Pass it even when the intent looks obvious, so the runtime default never decides.
	</AnnotatedStep>

	<AnnotatedStep meta="{11}" color="blue">
		The `run` handler. By the time it runs, the platform has already resolved "9 AM Eastern" on this date to the correct real instant using the zone's rules.
	</AnnotatedStep>

	<AnnotatedStep meta={`{12} "instantFromDate" "payload.timestamp"`} color="orange">
		The seam. `payload.timestamp` is a `Date` (UTC), not an ISO string, so you convert it with `instantFromDate`, not `Instant.from(...)`. From here on you hold a `Temporal.Instant`, and the `Date` is gone.
	</AnnotatedStep>

	<AnnotatedStep meta="{13}" color="blue">
		Everything downstream takes the `Instant`. The domain never sees the `Date`; it stopped at the seam one line up.
	</AnnotatedStep>
</AnnotatedCode>

:::caution
The SDK accepts a schedule with no `timezone`, but omitting it fires at the runtime's default zone: fine in dev, wrong in production. Name the zone on every wall-clock schedule, and name `'UTC'` explicitly on the ones meant to run in UTC.
:::

## Per-tenant schedules and the propagation problem

The static schedule is one global job. The SaaS-shaped case is different: each customer wants their report at 9 AM *in their own zone*. That's not one schedule but N schedules, one per user, each carrying that user's timezone. It also raises the hard part of this lesson: what happens to all that scheduled work when a user *changes* their timezone.

### Registering one schedule per tenant

"Send me my report at 9 AM in my zone" is a per-user schedule you create at runtime when the user opts in, not something you declare statically in code. That's `schedules.create`, and one shape detail trips everyone up: the object flips. In the static `schedules.task` form, `cron` is an object with `timezone` nested inside it. In `schedules.create`, `cron` is a plain **string** and `timezone` is a **top-level** field. Same concept, different shape, and the type checker won't always catch the mistake.

Note `externalId` and `deduplicationKey`: they're what make this idempotent. The `externalId` ties the schedule back to your user row, and the `deduplicationKey` means that if the opt-in handler runs twice, you get one schedule, not two.

<div data-mark-color="green">

```ts title="src/jobs/daily-summary.ts" {4,5}
export const enableDailySummary = async (user: User) => {
  await schedules.create({
    task: weeklySummary.id,
    cron: '0 9 * * 1-5',
    timezone: user.timeZone,
    externalId: user.id,
    deduplicationKey: `summary:${user.id}`,
  });
};

export const disableDailySummary = async (scheduleId: string) => {
  await schedules.del(scheduleId);
};
```

</div>

The two highlighted lines are the shape flip: `cron` is a plain string here, and `timezone` sits at the top level, the inverse of the nested object in the static form. On opt-out, `schedules.del(scheduleId)` removes the schedule (the v4 method is `del`, not `delete`). The id comes from the `create` result, or from a `schedules.list({ externalId })` lookup keyed on the user.

### What happens when the user changes their zone

A user moves from New York to Tokyo and updates the timezone on their profile, the exact surface from the profile lesson. What should happen to everything that depended on their old zone?

The mistake is treating "their scheduled stuff" as one undifferentiated bucket. It's three buckets, and they react to the change in three different ways.

**Future recurring schedules need to be re-registered with the new zone.** Their daily 9 AM summary should now fire at 9 AM *Tokyo* time. That schedule is still pending and still wall-clock-meaningful, so you update it: `schedules.update(scheduleId, { task, cron, timezone: newZone })`. Two shape notes, both echoing `create`: the first argument is a **schedule id** (resolve it from the `externalId` via the create result or `schedules.list`), and `update` takes `cron` as a plain string with a top-level `timezone`, the flat shape, *not* the nested object form. Skip this step and you've built the canonical "the profile timezone exists but the schedules still fire in the old zone" bug: the UI says Tokyo, and the report keeps arriving on New York's clock. The chain is simple once you see it: a profile-zone change means you recompute the schedule's zone.

**Future one-shot instants should be honored as they are.** A "remind me at 5 PM next Friday in my zone" job was converted to a fixed `Temporal.Instant` *at scheduling time*, back when the user was in New York. That instant is now a settled fact. Changing the profile zone does **not** retroactively shift it: a reminder scheduled in New York fires at the New-York-derived instant even after the user moves to Tokyo. The bug to avoid is the *opposite* of the last one, a silent retroactive shift that quietly drags every already-scheduled reminder an hour or three. If you want to offer a rebase, make it an explicit, opt-in prompt ("you have 4 scheduled reminders; update them to Tokyo time?"), never an automatic one.

**Already-fired past instants never change.** Last month's report sent when it sent. History is immutable, so there is nothing to propagate.

There's a legitimate alternative worth naming, because it changes the trade-off. Instead of storing the zone *on* each schedule and updating it on every profile change, you can have the job recompute the zone *from the user's profile on every fire*. Then profile changes propagate for free, with no `schedules.update` call to remember, because the schedule reads the live zone each time it runs. The cost is that every fire now does a profile read and the schedule's behavior is less self-contained. Both options are valid; choose deliberately.

The `externalId` is doing quiet work across all of this. It's the <Term definition="A caller-supplied key — here the user's or org's id — that ties a Trigger.dev schedule back to your own database row. It's how you find the schedule again later to update or delete it.">externalId</Term> that lets you find a user's schedule again to update its zone, or delete it on opt-out, without storing Trigger.dev's internal schedule id yourself.

## Disambiguation and idempotency keys

The scheduler handles DST for you on `cron`-driven schedules. Two cases it can't, and you own both.

### Choosing what happens at a gap or a repeat

Once you stop using a `cron` string and start *constructing* a wall-clock time yourself, with `someZonedDateTime.with({ hour: 9 })` or by building a `ZonedDateTime` to compute a one-shot fire instant, *you* decide what happens at a gap or a repeat. Temporal hands you that decision through a <Term definition="Temporal's option for which real instant a wall-clock time resolves to when DST makes that time impossible (a spring-forward gap) or ambiguous (a fall-back repeat).">disambiguation</Term> option, and you need two of its settings.

`disambiguation: 'compatible'` is the default. On a spring-forward **gap** it moves *forward* by the length of the gap, picking the later instant, so a request for the missing 2:30 AM resolves to 3:30 AM. On a fall-back **repeat** it picks the *earlier* of the two possible instants. That matches what most people expect, which is why you'll rarely override it.

`disambiguation: 'reject'` throws a `RangeError` the instant it hits a gap or a repeat, turning an ambiguous wall-clock time into a loud error instead of a silent guess. Reach for it when a fire that's wrong by an hour would be unacceptable and you'd rather the code stop than quietly pick. Temporal offers finer controls beyond these two for niche cases. If you schedule in the safe 9 AM band you never land in a gap or a repeat, so `disambiguation` never fires.

### Keying idempotency on the instant, not the wall clock

Every scheduled fire carries an idempotency key, from the background-work chapter. The DST-specific trap is in how you build it. Suppose you key off the wall-clock string: `` `${scheduleId}:2026-11-01 01:30` ``. On a fall-back day the 1:30 AM hour happens twice, both fires produce the *same* wall-clock string, so they hash to the *same* key, and one of two legitimate fires gets silently swallowed as a duplicate.

The fix is to key on the `Instant`, not the wall clock. Compare the two approaches below.

<CodeVariants>
	<CodeVariant label="Wall-clock key — drifts under fall-back">
		<div data-mark-color="red">

		```ts {2}
		const wallClock = fireZoned.toPlainDateTime().toString();
		const key = `${scheduleId}:${wallClock}`;
		```

		</div>
		**Two distinct fires collide.** Under fall-back, both 1:30 AM fires produce the same wall-clock string, so they share one key, and the second fire is silently dropped as a duplicate.
	</CodeVariant>

	<CodeVariant label="Instant key — structural">
		<div data-mark-color="green">

		```ts {2}
		const fireInstant = instantFromDate(payload.timestamp);
		const key = `${scheduleId}:${fireInstant.toString()}`;
		```

		</div>
		**Distinct instants, distinct keys.** The two 1:30 AM fires are different `Instant` values, so their keys differ. The repeated hour can't collide.
	</CodeVariant>
</CodeVariants>

Two instants that share a wall-clock representation under fall-back are still *different* `Instant` values, an hour apart on the timeline. Keying on the instant makes the collision structurally impossible: the distinction the type already carries does the disambiguation for you.

One operational rule: **never pre-compute a year of fire instants into a table.** The IANA timezone database, the <Term definition="The IANA Time Zone Database — the canonical, frequently-updated dataset of every zone's offset and DST rules, past and future. It ships bundled with the runtime, so you never hand-roll it.">tzdata</Term>, gets updated several times a year as countries change their DST rules, sometimes on short notice, so a November instant you computed in January can be wrong by the time November arrives. Compute only the *next* fire, at fire time, from the current data. The platform keeps `tzdata` current, so you never ship a hand-rolled timezone library to do its job.

## Cadence versus wall-clock, side by side

Here are the two homes for a recurring job, placed side by side.

<CodeVariants>
	<CodeVariant label="Internal cadence — UTC">
		```ts {3}
		export const retentionSweep = schedules.task({
		  id: 'retention-sweep',
		  cron: { pattern: '0 3 * * *', timezone: 'UTC' },
		  run: async () => {
		    const cutoff = Temporal.Now
		      .zonedDateTimeISO('UTC')
		      .subtract({ days: 30 })
		      .toInstant();
		    await deleteExpired({ before: cutoff });
		  },
		});
		```
		**Cadence, not wall clock.** No human cares that it runs at 3 AM UTC, only that it runs nightly, so it's UTC, stated explicitly. The 30-day subtraction happens on a `ZonedDateTime` and then converts to an `Instant`, because calendar-unit math needs a calendar.
	</CodeVariant>

	<CodeVariant label="User-facing — named zone">
		```ts {3-6}
		export const weeklySummary = schedules.task({
		  id: 'weekly-summary',
		  cron: {
		    pattern: '0 9 * * 1-5',
		    timezone: 'America/New_York',
		  },
		  run: async (payload) => {
		    const fireInstant = instantFromDate(payload.timestamp);
		    await buildSummaryFor({ instant: fireInstant });
		  },
		});
		```
		**Wall clock, not cadence.** A human reads this at 9 AM their time, year-round, so name the zone and let the scheduler handle DST.
	</CodeVariant>
</CodeVariants>

Wall-clock-meaningful jobs name a zone, cadence jobs run in UTC, and you pass the zone explicitly either way so the runtime default never decides when your code fires.
